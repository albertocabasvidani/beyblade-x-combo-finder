Cobalt Dragoon 5-60 Elevate doesn't compete on endurance. That is the point that sets its logic apart from almost everything else in the roster: most attack combos try to hit harder or more often before they run out of spin, hoping the accumulated damage is enough before the rotation drops. Cobalt Dragoon does something different, and dirtier: it spins in the opposite direction (left-spin) to the vast majority of the meta, and every contact with a right-spin opponent produces opposite-spin erosion that takes away rotation literally, not through accumulated friction but through the direct collision of the two spin directions.

The Cobalt Dragoon blade (atk60/def15/sta25) is tuned for pure attack, not for absorbing hits: the stat profile says it plainly, with defense as the lowest of the three parameters. The 5-60 ratchet raises the center of gravity and slightly lengthens the travel, but it is the Elevate bit that makes the mechanism workable in practice: it keeps the combo mobile and aggressive enough to maximize the number of contacts, which is the real currency of this strategy. You don't need to win every exchange overwhelmingly — you need to pile up contacts that steal rotation, one after another, until the opponent no longer has enough spin to stay in the stadium. It is also a different logic from slower erosion combos built on bits like [Shark Scale](/parts/shark-scale/), which work on friction over time rather than on stealing rotation directly at contact.

On this site the combo scores 8.68/10, with performance 0.93, presence 0.787 and corroboration 0.831. These numbers are built on our own evidence window: 442 tournament events, 160 wins, 527 top cut appearances and 2.4% meta share in the last month, played mostly on Xtreme and Infinity stadiums.

The most interesting August 2026 data point, though, is the trajectory, not the absolute level. In our dataset, placements went from 8 to 24 between July and August — almost tripled — the sharpest rise among all the combos tracked that month. It is a sign of adoption, not of a single lucky event: more players brought it to tournaments, and the results followed. It has to be kept separate from the public WBO all-time ranking, an independent scoring system that puts Cobalt Dragoon 5-60 Elevate at #3 of all time with 818 WBO points and 717 wins according to that ranking. They are two different measures, over different windows and methodologies: our 160 is not comparable to the WBO's 717, and the two numbers must never be merged into a single figure.

The clearest case where the left-spin steal mechanism can be seen at work is the matchup against [Wizard Rod 1-60 Hexa](/combos/wizard-rod-1-60-hexa/), score 9.62 and #1 WBO all-time — the dominant stamina combo of the current format. In a duel of pure endurance, [Wizard Rod](/parts/wizard-rod/) almost always wins: it spins longer, survives longer, and most of the roster's attack simply can't close it out before the opponent's stamina collapses on its own. Cobalt Dragoon 5-60 Elevate is described as the only blade that beats it reliably in that specific matchup, and the reason lies exactly in the mechanism: it doesn't try to outlast Wizard Rod, because on that ground it would lose. It neutralizes it by stealing rotation at every contact, sidestepping the stamina comparison instead of confronting it head-on. It is the only known strategy in the format that works against that matchup, and it explains why a combo with a moderate attack profile can beat the strongest stamina combo in the roster.

The results of September 19, 2026 show the same strength applied at different scales. At Dallas Beyblade Association - Breaking Limits - Limited #8, a local tournament with 7 players, Cobalt Dragoon 5-60 Elevate won first place. On the same day, at Dallas Beyblade Association - The Beycon Blitz - Standard#180, a more substantial event with 17 players, it placed second and third — two blades of the same combo in the top cut of the same tournament. The data proves nothing on an isolated event — a 7-player tournament is statistically weak on its own — but the combination of the two results on the same day, on brackets of different size, is what matters: the combo doesn't only win when the field is small, it holds up when the number of players, and therefore the variety of opponents, grows. It is the kind of signal that accompanies real growth in adoption, not an isolated peak.
